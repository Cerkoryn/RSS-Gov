Methods#

What RSS-Gov claims, what it does not, and how each number is produced. The first section is a plain-language summary; the rest is the detail behind it.

In short#

The question. DReps, and the people who delegate to them, could be paid for taking part in governance: from Cardano's treasury, or by a proposer fee: part of each governance action's deposit kept instead of returned. There are many ways to do that, and each one buys something and risks something. RSS-Gov does not pick a winner. It measures what each way of paying does, on five things that matter, so that the choice between them can be made in the open.

The five things measured. For every reward rule:

  1. Participation: how much of all delegated ADA ends up casting a vote.
  2. Decentralisation: how many DReps it takes to hold two thirds of the voting power. More is better: power is spread wider.
  3. Skin in the game: how much of the voting power is backed by the DRep's own ADA, its pledge, so that those who decide also bear the consequences.
  4. Cost: how much ADA a year the rewards cost, whoever pays it.
  5. Sybil risk: how much of the budget someone gains by pretending to be several DReps (a Sybil attack). Lower is better.

Only votes are paid. Every rule pays for explicit votes (Yes, No and Abstain alike). Nothing is paid for just being registered or holding delegation.

What is known and what is assumed. Some things can be computed exactly from the chain today: who a rule would pay, what it costs, and how much splitting into several DReps would gain. How people react to being paid cannot be known before anyone has been paid. So RSS-Gov does three things:

  • It builds a model of governance without rewards from the chain's history: how often each DRep votes, how delegators move, how DReps come and go. It then checks that model by starting it 40 epochs in the past and seeing whether it reproduces what actually happened. It does.
  • It adds four reactions to money, each switched on and off separately: DReps vote more, delegators move towards paying DReps, DReps spend awards on attracting delegation, and DReps add pledge when a rule rewards it. Each is tried at a low, a central and a high strength. A result that only appears at high strength is a hope, not a finding.
  • It runs every rule many times from the same starting point with the same luck, and reports each result as a difference from paying nothing, with its uncertainty.

How a simulation works, below, explains each step in plain terms, with diagrams.

What comes out. No rule wins on every count. Paying in proportion to voting power draws more votes but, if awards help DReps attract delegation, concentrates power. Paying everyone the same invites fake DReps. Caps and "skip the largest" invite large DReps to split themselves. Paying delegators moves delegation toward the DReps they are paid through, which spreads power or concentrates it depending on which DReps those are. Tying pay to pledge shows how little pledge stands behind today's votes. The results page shows these trade-offs side by side, and which designs are not beaten on all five at once.

What would settle it. Only a real programme can measure how strongly people react. The last section describes how a programme could be set up to measure it.

How to take part. Anyone can propose a reward rule on the "Propose a scheme" page; it is simulated exactly like the others and listed with its results. The method itself is open too: this page is generated from METHODS.md, and CONTRIBUTING.md explains how to suggest a change.

The question#

Every way of paying for governance participation buys something and risks something. Paying in proportion to voting power rewards the DReps who already hold the power. Paying everyone the same invites fake DReps. Paying delegators can move idle ADA but spreads the money thin. Ignoring a DRep's pledge rewards power exercised with nothing at risk. The question is therefore not "which rule is best" but: for each rule, what does it do on each of these dimensions, how sure are we, and which rules are not beaten on all of them at once?

Nobody has been paid yet, so history cannot say how people react to money. RSS-Gov separates three kinds of evidence and never mixes them:

Tier Question Depends on
A. Static Who would this rule pay today, what does it cost, how gameable is it? Chain data only
B. Model What happens if people react with a given strength? Chain data + assumed reaction strengths
C. Real world How strongly do people actually react? A real programme set up as an experiment

Tier A results are facts about a formula. Tier B results are conditional forecasts. Only tier C can turn them into findings.

How a simulation works#

This section explains in plain terms what happens inside one simulated programme. The sections after it give the formulas and where each number comes from.

One run#

A run is one possible six months of Cardano governance (36 epochs of five days) under one reward rule. It starts from the chain as it is today: every DRep with voting power, its voting record and pledge, and the accounts delegating to it. Large accounts are copied one by one and small ones as weighted samples, so that a few tens of thousands of rows stand for every delegating account. The run then plays the epochs forward one at a time, in six steps:

The legend shows which parts copy how governance behaved without rewards, which part is the rule under test, and which parts are the assumed reactions to money. With every reaction switched off, a paid run is identical to an unpaid one.

How DReps behave#

Without rewards, everything a DRep does copies its own history:

  • Voting. Each DRep has its own chance of voting on an action, learned from its record. Recent votes count more than old ones, and a DRep with a short record is pulled toward the typical rate of DReps of its size. Each action is a real past action picked at random, and it keeps the attention it drew: on a busy action every DRep is more likely to vote. Whether a DRep votes is then a weighted coin toss. Votes split into Yes, No and Abstain in the proportions the real action got; the model does not try to predict what DReps think of a proposal.
  • Vote rate. Rules pay by a DRep's vote rate: the share of the actions closed in the last 12 epochs that it voted on. A DRep that stops voting earns nothing within 12 epochs.
  • Coming and going. New DReps register at the historical rate. Each starts with a delegation, voting habits and pledge drawn from those of real recent newcomers. DReps retire at the historical rate; the one that retires is the one closest in size to a DRep that really retired. Its delegators are left undelegated until they choose again.

With rewards, a DRep can react in the ways below, each only when its response strength is above zero. Every reaction is worked out afresh each epoch from what the rule pays at that moment; none builds up on its own.

  • Voting more (drep_response). A DRep looks at what one more vote would earn it: its award if it voted on every action, divided by the number of actions. The higher that is next to the effort of a vote, put at 50 ADA, the more its chance of voting rises. For example, at the central strength a DRep that votes on half of the actions votes on about 59% when a vote pays 50 ADA, and about 76% when it pays 450 ADA. Doubling the pay does not double the effect. A DRep the rule would not pay, because it is too small or among the largest that are skipped, sees no gain and does not change.
  • Writing rationales (the same strength). The same reaction, for rules that pay extra for a rationale, with the effort of a rationale put at 100 ADA.
  • Registering (the same strength). If a newcomer of typical size would be paid well next to the effort of voting on everything, more people register, up to eight times the historical rate.
  • Buying reach (reinvest_response). A DRep can spend its award on promotion, tools or staff, which makes it more visible to delegators who are choosing. At the central strength, an award equal to the cost of voting on everything makes it 15% more likely to be chosen. Under a rule that pays by voting power this helps the largest DReps most, which is how a rule can concentrate power.
  • Adding pledge (pledge_response), under rules that count pledge: see below.

How delegators behave#

Without rewards:

  • Balances drift. Each account grows or shrinks a little every epoch, at the pace and with the ups and downs seen on chain. Accounts with always-abstain have their own pace.
  • Most accounts never move. In each epoch an account re-delegates with a small probability, learned from how long today's delegations have lasted. Accounts of 100,000 ADA or more have their own rate.
  • Where movers go. A mover chooses always-abstain, no-confidence or a DRep, in the proportions recent movers chose them. Among DReps it favours large ones and active ones, with strengths fitted to history so that the spread of voting power keeps to its real path. Because movers favour DReps that vote, a DRep that votes more because it is paid also gains delegation. That link is fitted, not assumed.

With rewards (delegator_response), only when a rule pays delegators, they chase yield in two ways. An account earning less than a typical paying DRep offers becomes more likely to move: at the central strength, twice as likely when the gap is one point a year, and never more than ten times. And a mover favours DReps that pay more: at the central strength one point of yield a year makes a DRep about 2.7 times as likely to be chosen. When a DRep shares a fixed award with its delegators, each newcomer lowers the yield for the next, so movers are taken in small batches and the yields are updated in between.

Delegators do not react to what a DRep itself is paid, except through the reach the DRep buys with it.

Pledge#

Two words are used throughout. A DRep's voting power is all the ADA delegated to it, its own included. Its pledge is the part of that ADA it owns itself. The word is borrowed from stake pools, where an operator's pledge is the ADA it puts into its own pool; "stake" is kept for stake pools.

The ledger has no idea of a DRep's pledge, so the simulation uses what can be traced on chain: the accounts that paid for the DRep's registration and that it names in its metadata. Of the ADA in those accounts:

  • the part delegated to the DRep itself is its pledge. It stands behind the DRep's votes and is part of its voting power like any other delegated ADA;
  • the whole balance, wherever it is delegated, is the DRep's holdings: a measure of what it could afford, used only to limit top-ups;
  • ADA that others delegate to the DRep is voting power and is never counted as pledge.

New DReps get the pledge of a real recent newcomer, no more than their voting power, and their holdings are taken to be that pledge.

What the chain cannot show. For most small DReps the trace works: many delegate their own wallet to themselves, and it makes up most of their voting power. For most of the largest it does not. Their linked accounts are usually a small operating wallet holding a few hundred ADA or less, and following every registration, update and vote transaction leads back to the same wallet. Several of the largest DReps are organisations (wallets, staking providers, ecosystem companies) whose own ADA sits in accounts nothing on chain ties to them. A DRep's large delegators cannot be attributed either: the same account sizes recur across DReps (delegation programmes), and staking providers delegate their clients' ADA. Two other ways of estimating were tried and rejected. A line fitted through pledge against voting power explains little of the spread, predicts very different things depending on which way it is fitted, and is fitted on the same small wallets, so it inherits their bias; stake pools, whose pledge the ledger enforces, show the same weak link between own ADA and size. A larger pledge_wealth_multiple only scales the traced balances, so it helps the few DReps whose real wallets were found and leaves the rest with almost nothing.

So what DReps own beyond what can be traced is an assumption, untraced_wealth_share: a DRep's holdings are taken to be at least that share of its voting power at the start, 0 at low (only what can be traced), 0.1% at central and 1% at high. At 0.1% a DRep with 100M ADA of voting power is taken to own at least 100,000 ADA; at 1% the limit on top-ups hardly binds any more, so the high scenario is close to no limit at all. Only holdings change: a DRep's starting pledge, and so today's backing, stay at what can be traced, a floor. In a pilot DReps would declare their accounts, and own ADA revealed that way would show up as added pledge.

What depends on it: with more untraced wealth, large DReps can add pledge under rules tied to it, so those rules raise backing more and spread voting power less, and a pledge hard cap takes far less voting power away. Designs that pay no attention to pledge are unaffected.

A rule can use pledge in three ways. A minimum pledge (min_pledge_ada) pays only DReps with at least that much, and their delegators. Or, set by a leverage L:

  • A pledge soft cap, for pay. Only up to L times its pledge counts toward the DRep's reward. With L = 2, a DRep with 10M ADA of voting power and 1M of pledge is paid as if it had 2M. It still votes with all 10M.
  • A pledge hard cap on voting power itself: that DRep would vote with 2M. This needs a change to the ledger.

Topping up pledge#

Under such a rule a DRep may add pledge, when pledge_response is above zero. It decides once an epoch:

  1. What is missing. The pledge it needs so that all of its voting power counts, or under a cap on counted power all of the capped amount, minus what it has. If nothing is missing, it adds nothing.
  2. This epoch's step. A small share of the gap, sized so that if nothing else changed the DRep would close pledge_response of it over the programme: a quarter at the central strength.
  3. What it can afford. Its pledge can grow to at most pledge_wealth_multiple times its holdings. Surveys of crypto owners find crypto is on average about a fifth of their financial wealth, more than half of it for about a fifth of owners, and under 15% for most; hence 5 times at the central strength, 2 at low and 7 at high. Holdings are what its linked accounts hold, or untraced_wealth_share of its voting power if that is more (see What the chain cannot show); at low, a DRep with no linked accounts adds nothing.
  4. Whether it pays. The extra reward a year the step would bring, with everyone else as they are, must be at least pledge_min_return of the ADA added: 5% at the central strength. Under a rule that pays everyone the same, pledge earns nothing and nobody adds any. Under a hard cap the test is skipped: the DRep adds pledge to keep its voting power, whatever it earns.

ADA a DRep adds counts as its pledge from then on and joins its voting power, so it votes. The model treats it as new ADA brought into governance, not taken from another DRep, and it leaves with the DRep if the DRep retires.

A minimum pledge does not trigger top-ups: a DRep below it is taken to stay below it. A real DRep close to the minimum might add the rest, so the model understates how many would qualify.

How the five measures are taken#

Four of the five measures come out of each run. The fifth, Sybil risk, is exact and needs no simulation.

  1. Participation. On every action that closes during the run: the voting power of the DReps who voted on it (Yes, No or Abstain alike), divided by all delegated ADA: to DReps (including pledge they added), to always-abstain or to no-confidence, or left behind by a DRep that retired. It is averaged over all the actions in the run. Because always-abstain is in the denominator, moving ADA from it to a DRep that votes raises participation, while a DRep that already votes always cannot.
  2. Decentralisation. At the end of each epoch, DReps are lined up from the largest down, and the measure is how many it takes to pass two thirds of all DRep voting power (after any hard cap). It is averaged over the second half of the programme, when rewards have had time to move delegation.
  3. Skin in the game. At the end of each epoch: for each DRep, the part of its voting power covered by its pledge, including any top-ups; summed over DReps and divided by all DRep voting power. A DRep with more pledge than voting power does not make up for another's shortfall. It is averaged over the second half of the programme.
  4. Cost. All ADA paid out during the run, to DReps and to delegators, plus about 0.002 ADA for each payment (payout_fee_ada), turned into ADA a year. Each recipient gets one payment for the whole programme, however small (see How payments are made). Money a rule does not pay out is not a cost.
  5. Sybil risk. The largest share of the budget someone could gain by splitting one identity into several DReps, computed from the rule and today's chain without a simulation: see Tier A.

From runs to results#

Each rule is run many times. Run number 1 of every rule, and of paying nothing, sees the same luck: the same actions closing in the same epochs, the same would-be newcomers, the same coin tosses for who moves and who votes. Any difference between a rule's run and the unpaid run with the same luck is therefore mostly the rule's doing. A result is the average of those differences over all runs, with a 95% interval, and it is computed three times: with weak, central and strong reactions. A result that holds at all three is one the assumptions do not decide.

The axes#

One measure per objective family. Decision quality (and "harmful proposals") is left out: it cannot be observed on chain, and any label for it would be invented. Proposal completion is left out as an axis too: too few actions close during one programme to tell schemes apart on it. The model still simulates ratification, which is checked against history and reported as supported_ratified in the run tables.

Family Measure (config.yaml name) Better Tier
Participation Share of all delegated ADA, including always-abstain and no-confidence, that casts an explicit vote on an action (participation) higher B
Decentralisation DReps needed to exceed two thirds of DRep voting power (tau67) higher B
Skin in the game Share of DRep voting power backed by the DRep's pledge: Σ min(power, pledge) / Σ power (backing) higher B, from an on-chain start
Reward sustainability Cost a year: what is paid out, from a budget or from fees, plus a small fee per payment (annual_cost_ada) lower A
Sybil resistance Largest share of the budget gained by splitting one identity into several (split_gain) lower A

Notes on the choices:

  • Participation is measured against all delegated ADA, not only ADA already with DReps. A scheme that moves ADA out of always-abstain into a voting DRep therefore scores, and one that only makes existing DReps vote more scores less than its turnout suggests. Turnout among DReps is still reported.
  • Skin in the game is the reciprocal of leverage. One DRep's surplus cannot cover another's shortfall. Today it is under 1%: see the data section for why that number is a proxy.
  • Cost is an axis because the budget is a design choice. Named schemes are compared at one budget; the search varies it, and variations of named designs at other budgets show what more money buys; each named design is also run paid by fees alone. It counts every ADA paid out, whether it comes from a budget or from a proposer fee, and does not say who bears it in the end.
  • Sybil resistance is a measured quantity, not a pass/fail gate. Like cost, it is an exact property of a rule and is traded off against the other axes with no limit of its own. Its scale in config.yaml sets how fast it counts against a design in the hypervolume: a rule that lets splitting gain half the budget or more covers nothing on it.

Each axis has a meaningful difference in config.yaml: the smallest change worth acting on. A change smaller than that counts as "same as unpaid" even if it is statistically clear, and a change larger than that counts only if its 95% interval excludes zero.

Data#

rss-gov data pull reads the public Koios API and stores parquet tables under data/: governance actions and their official tallies, every vote, each DRep's voting power in every epoch since epoch 508, DRep certificates, protocol parameters, treasury totals, and the accounts currently delegating to each DRep and to the two predefined options.

Two delegator lists are too long to page through (always-abstain has about 200,000 accounts). For lists over 5,000 accounts, every account of at least 100,000 ADA is fetched exactly and the rest are represented by evenly spaced sample pages with a weight. Accounts of at least 100,000 ADA hold about 90% of delegated ADA.

A DRep's pledge is not a ledger concept. Two clues are on chain and both are downloaded: the account that funded the DRep's registration transaction, and the payment address the DRep declares in its metadata. The current balance of those accounts, each counted once, is what the DRep is known to hold. Only the part delegated to the DRep itself counts as its pledge: own ADA delegated elsewhere is not behind its votes, and ADA others delegate to it is never counted as pledge. This is a proxy with errors in both directions: a DRep can hold ADA in accounts never linked to it, a registration can be paid for by someone else, and a declared address proves nothing about control. It is good enough to show the order of magnitude (most large DReps vote with thousands of times their pledge) and not good enough to pay on. A programme that rewards pledge needs accounts declared and signed for by the DRep key.

Raw pages are cached, so a pull can be rebuilt offline; manifest.json records a hash per table and calibration.json copies those hashes.

The reward family (mechanisms.py)#

Only explicit votes are paid. Yes, No and Abstain count alike; not voting earns nothing; nothing is paid for merely being registered or holding delegation. Each epoch a DRep's weight is

eligible × rank × size^alpha × vote rate × (1 + bonus × rationale share)

where vote rate is the share of actions closed in the last 12 epochs that the DRep voted on, and size is its voting power, limited by cap and by leverage × pledge, or, with basis: delegators, its number of delegating accounts. Weight is linear in the vote rate, so every vote earns the same increment.

  • eligible applies a minimum voting power in ADA, a minimum share of all DRep voting power (min_share), a minimum number of delegators, a minimum pledge (min_pledge_ada) and a minimum vote rate.
  • rank handles "skip the K largest, pay the next N". The K largest are weighted by top_weight (0 skips them; 0.25 pays them a quarter). With roll_down, a place among the N passes down when a DRep above does not vote: a DRep keeps the part of a place left after the DReps ranked above it are expected to vote (their vote rates), which is the expected value of paying the first N voters on each action.

The weights split the DReps' pot. equal_share splits that fraction of it by vote rate alone, and the rest by the full weight. Two settings stop the pot being spent in full; the unspent ADA stays in the budget:

  • per_vote_ada pays a fixed amount per vote instead of a share. Votes are the vote rate times the actions closing per epoch on average. If the votes would cost more than the pot, every payment is scaled down. It needs alpha 0 and no delegator share.
  • max_award_share limits any one DRep to that share of the DReps' pot. The excess is not handed to the others.

A share of the pot can go to delegators, always through a DRep that voted: passthrough shares each DRep's own award with its delegators, flat pays one yield per ADA and per unit of the DRep's vote rate. Delegating to always-abstain or to a DRep that does not vote earns nothing. delegator_k_exclude pays nothing to the delegators of the K DReps with the most voting power, though those DReps can still be paid themselves; the delegators' part is shared among the delegators of the other paid DReps. Under pass-through, a pledge soft cap also lowers what a DRep's delegators get: each ADA they delegate earns in proportion to how much of the DRep's voting power its pledge covers.

How payments are made. Payments go straight into reward accounts as direct deposits, which CIP-159 adds to the ledger: no transaction output, so no minimum amount, and nothing for a recipient to claim. CIP-159 comes with a hard fork; every design here assumes it is live, and every recipient is paid however little it earned. pilot_testable marks the designs that need no ledger change beyond it. Payments are scripted in batches; each deposit adds about 40 bytes to a transaction, about 0.002 ADA in fees.

leverage ties the voting power that counts for rewards to the DRep's pledge: a pledge soft cap. With hard_cap it limits voting power itself: a pledge hard cap. That changes ratification and needs a hard fork; such schemes are simulated and flagged pilot_testable: false.

Paying from a proposer fee#

fee_ada, the proposer fee, funds the pot from governance actions instead of, or as well as, a budget: each action that closes adds that much, as if that part of its proposer's deposit were kept as a fee rather than returned (CIP PR #1117 proposes this). Today every deposit is returned, so a fee needs a ledger change and such designs are flagged pilot_testable: false. With budget_ada: 0 a design is paid by fees alone.

The money arrives in lumps: many epochs close no action, and a busy one closes many at once. Payments follow the lumps, so the cost follows the number of actions in each run. Behaviour follows the steady flow, the fee times the average number of actions closing per epoch, because that is what a DRep or delegator can count on; reacting to each lump would make a fee look worse only because the model's actors look one epoch ahead. At the same expected money a fee therefore buys the same reactions as a budget, at a cost that moves with the number of actions.

Not modelled: proposers proposing less when part of the deposit is kept (the deposit has not changed since governance began, so there is no data on how they would react), fees added to treasury requests, and fewer actions easing the load on DReps (a DRep's chance of voting does not depend on how many actions are open). The number of actions in a run is drawn from history whatever the fee.

To compare the two sources, every named design is also simulated with no budget, paid by each proposer fee in experiment.fees_ada in config.yaml (named like capped_fee_50k, labelled "50k ADA proposer fee"). The report sets each beside its budget version. The search varies budgets, not fees, and does not start from these.

Named designs#

Besides the generic shapes (proportional, equal, sqrt, capped, gates, floors, rationale bonus, delegator designs and pledge caps), the named designs come from published proposals. Each is the closest point in the family, not an exact copy. The pledge_soft_cap_* designs were found by hand: they have only two settings each, so they are easy to explain and to implement. The delegator designs at the end of the table pay delegators to move away from the largest DReps, by pledge, by rank or both; config.yaml also runs variations of them (other leverages, no cap, half to delegators, the top 10 instead of the top 20, a pledge soft cap, and a minimum pledge of 10,000 ADA).

What the family cannot express#

  • Pay that depends on how a DRep voted: rewarding the winning side (Beccuti & Schneider), prediction-market accuracy scores, penalties for the losing side. The model draws vote direction from history and has no "correct" outcome, so this would reward conformity, not judgement.
  • Judged quality: peer-review juries, retroactive rewards, rubric scores, delegator ratings. None is computable from the chain; a rationale anchor is the closest.
  • Linking DReps run by one operator: the chain has no identity.
  • Delegator mechanics: time-weighted balances, one-time bonuses for leaving always-abstain or a large DRep, matching new delegation, caps per delegator, and a pool that grows with participation. Each needs a response assumption with no data behind it.
  • A base for everyone plus a bonus for the top m (IO Research variant 3): the family applies one eligibility rule to the whole pot.

Pay per action is approximated per epoch: roll_down uses expected votes, and per_vote_ada uses the average number of actions per epoch.

Tier A: static analysis (static.py)#

The rule is applied to today's real DReps with no behaviour change. Reported: how many are paid, the size of awards, the share going to the ten largest DReps, how many delegating accounts would be paid, the voting power a hard cap would leave, and two splitting measures. A proposer fee is counted at the average pace of closing actions in history; fee_share is the share of the money it brings in.

An outsider splitting. Someone brings a fixed amount of its own ADA (default 1M ADA) and spreads it over m fake DReps, each paying its deposit, voting on everything, attaching a rationale to every vote, holding its ADA in as many minimum-size accounts as the rule demands, and counting it as pledge. Reported: the share of the budget at the best m up to 5,000, and how much of that exceeds what one honest DRep of the same size gets. Only the excess is a Sybil gain; the rest is the reward for taking part.

An insider splitting. For each of the 20 largest DReps and three smaller ones: what it adds by splitting into 2, 5, 10 or 20 equal DReps with its delegators and pledge following. The largest gain is reported. Delegators may not follow, so this is an upper bound. Stake pool operators did split pools in response to a cap, so it is not a theoretical one.

split_gain is the larger of the two. Rules linear in voting power give nothing to split for. Equal splits reward an outsider's fake DReps. Caps, concave rules and "skip the largest" reward an insider's split, which is the less obvious half of the problem.

Tier B: the model (model.py)#

A run follows the six steps in How a simulation works. State: every DRep with voting power, and about 64,000 account rows that stand for the 270,000 delegating accounts. Details not given there:

  • The reward round uses what the chain shows at the start of the epoch, before that epoch's moves and votes. Money a round does not pay out carries over to the next.
  • A mover picks always-abstain, no-confidence, or a DRep with weight (voting power + floor)^theta × exp(beta × vote rate), times the reactions in the table below.
  • Each action is a whole historical action resampled (its threshold, the support it had among voters, its abstention share and the attention it drew), so the links between those stay as observed. A DRep votes with its own fitted probability, shifted on the log-odds scale by the action's attention.
  • Ratification follows the ledger rule for DReps: yes power over active power, with non-voters counted as no, abstain votes and inactive DReps removed, and no-confidence ADA counted as no. On history this rule alone reproduces 119 of 120 outcomes.

SPOs and the Constitutional Committee are not modelled. The scope is DReps and their delegators.

What is fitted, and from what (calibrate.py)#

Parameter Source
Each DRep's vote probability Its own recency-weighted vote history, shrunk toward DReps of similar size. The half-life is chosen by how well votes are predicted 18 epochs ahead.
Attention per action The logit shift that reconciles that forecast with the action's real turnout
Re-delegation rate, large and small accounts Age of current delegations in the account table
Share of moves to abstain / no-confidence Where recent movers went
Preference for active DReps (beta) Maximum likelihood on where large recent movers went
Preference for large DReps (theta) Simulated moments: the value that keeps the effective number of DReps on its observed path over the fit window
Balance drift and volatility Simulated moments: end totals and the turnover of DRep power shares
DRep entry and exit Counts and sizes over the last 80 epochs

theta deserves a note. Fitting it to individual movers' choices gives 0.45–0.80, and a model run with that value slowly dissolves concentration, which has not happened on chain: 20–26 DReps have held two thirds of the power throughout. Real inflows are lumpier than a size rule (the five most-chosen DReps take 40–60% of moving accounts), so theta is fitted to the concentration path instead and the cross-section value is kept as a diagnostic.

Validation gate#

The model is fitted on epochs T-79..T-40 only, started from the observed state at T-40, and run 40 times without rewards to T. It passes when simulated concentration, totals, DRep count, share turnover, turnout, ballots per action and the ratification rate of supported actions are within the tolerances in config.yaml. rss-gov calibrate prints each check; every later command warns if the gate failed.

Honest limits of this test:

  • The tolerances were set while building the model, not before. The next data pull is the first fully out-of-sample check: rerun rss-gov calibrate and see whether it still passes.
  • The account table exists only for today, so the re-delegation rate uses information from the validation window. For earlier epochs each DRep's observed total is split into accounts the way it is split today.
  • Total ADA delegated to DReps passes narrowly. One large account leaving moved it by 8% inside the window; the model cannot foresee such an event.
  • The raw ratification rate is reported but not gated: it depends on which proposals get submitted, and recent proposals were more contested than earlier ones.

What is assumed: four response channels#

Each trade-off has its own channel, so a result can be traced to the assumption it rests on. With every channel at zero, paying changes nothing (the tests check this).

Channel What it does Trade-off it drives
drep_response A DRep's log-odds of voting rise by response × log(1 + reward for one more vote / vote cost). The same strength scales rationale publishing and new registrations. Participation
delegator_response A mover's log-weight for a DRep rises by response per point of annual yield; accounts earning less than a typical paying DRep offers become more likely to move. Yield falls as delegation arrives. Mobilising idle ADA; where it goes
reinvest_response A DRep's pull on movers is multiplied by (1 + award / upkeep)^response: awards pay for reach. Under a power-proportional rule this adds about response to the exponent with which movers already favour large DReps. Concentration under proportional pay
pledge_response Under a rule that ties rewards or voting power to pledge, a DRep closes this share of its missing pledge over the programme. Under a pledge soft cap, only the pledge that would count for rewards is missing (no more than any cap on counted power allows), and a DRep adds each part only while the extra award it brings is at least pledge_min_return a year on that ADA. Under a pledge hard cap a DRep adds it to keep its voting power, whatever it earns. Either way its pledge grows to at most pledge_wealth_multiple times its holdings (the ADA in its linked accounts, or untraced_wealth_share of its voting power if more), a bound on what it could buy, and the ADA it adds joins its voting power. Skin in the game

low, central and high move all four together. voting_only, yield_only, reinvest_only and pledge_only switch on one channel at its central value; the report uses them to show which assumption each result depends on. A scheme's score for ranking is its mean over low, central and high. The central values are guesses. A design is credible only if its verdicts hold from low to high.

Not modelled: delegators leaving a DRep whose voting power is capped, vote buying, collusion, changes in vote direction or quality, and price effects.

A DRep reacts to what one more vote is worth at its current size. It does not campaign for delegation to reach a threshold. Rules that pay only above a size (threshold_k, top-m) are therefore judged without the climb they are meant to cause, beyond what movers already give DReps that vote more.

Comparing schemes fairly (experiment.py)#

  1. Same start. Every scheme starts from the same chain snapshot.
  2. Same money. Named schemes get the same budget and programme length. Where budget varies (variations, the search) cost is an axis, so a cheaper design is not penalised for buying less.
  3. Same luck. All schemes share seeds, and every random draw about the outside world (which actions arrive, who would register, which accounts move) is common across schemes within a seed. Effects are mean paired differences from unpaid with a 95% interval across seeds.
  4. Same yardstick. Each axis gets a verdict against unpaid: better, worse, or no meaningful difference. Verdicts are computed per scenario; the report marks a verdict as solid only when it holds in low, central and high.
  5. Measurements apart from judgement. The axes are measured. Where to draw lines is in guardrails: a design is acceptable when no axis other than cost and Sybil resistance is worse than unpaid in any scenario. Those two are exact trade-offs every paid rule makes to some degree, so they are weighed on the front instead. Changing a guardrail re-labels designs without re-running anything.
  6. No single score. A design is shortlisted when no other acceptable design is at least as good on every axis and meaningfully better on one. Designs outside a guardrail are not discarded: they are grouped by exactly which downside they accept, and the unbeaten ones in each group are reported (best_of_its_kind). If nothing is inside every guardrail, those groups are the result. What remains is a choice between trade-offs, which is a governance decision, not a modelling one.

Hard-fork-only designs go through the same comparison and get their own shortlist. They cannot be tried without a ledger change, so for them tiers A and B are all the evidence there will be before one.

Search and hypervolume (search.py)#

An evolutionary search over the family, budget included, with no language model in the loop. Each generation mutates designs on the current front and adds random ones.

A search over noisy simulations always finds something. So the finalists are re-run on 100 seeds the search never saw, and only those numbers are reported.

With five axes almost any design is unbeaten on something, so the front is thinned: two designs that differ by less than the meaningful amount on every axis count as one.

Hypervolume is used for one thing: a single number for "how much of the trade-off space does this set of designs cover". Each axis is rescaled to 0–1 between the scale bounds in config.yaml, expressed as a difference from unpaid, and the volume is exact. Unpaid is always a member, since doing nothing is always available. The 95% interval comes from resampling seeds. The report also gives what the search adds over the named schemes; if that interval includes zero, the search found nothing the named schemes did not offer.

Hypervolume depends on the chosen scales and rewards coverage of every corner, including corners nobody wants (a very cheap programme that does nothing much). It tracks search progress. It does not rank designs: read the shortlist.

Proposed designs: community and agents#

Every design is a point in one family, so every setting of every design can be changed. The site lists the settings each design was simulated with, and gives a design that differs from a named one in one or two settings a short name of that kind ("Middle out · skip 20"). Variations listed in config.yaml (a named design with some settings changed) are simulated like the named designs.

Anyone can propose a design in the same family, through the "Propose a scheme" page or as a YAML file. A proposal gets the exact static checks at once (the page computes them in the browser with the same formulas, and a test keeps the two implementations equal) and is then simulated on the holdout seeds by rss-gov try, so its numbers are directly comparable with the search finalists. A proposal submitted as a GitHub issue is simulated and published as soon as it arrives, with no human step, up to three a day per person. Every proposal is kept in results/proposals/ with its verdict and its runs, and shown on the results page whether or not it joins the front. The site titles it from its rule and links to the issue for the idea behind it rather than repeating the issue's text. Proposals simulated under an older calibration are marked as needing a rerun.

An AI agent can propose designs the same way, following program.md. Only rss-gov research --rounds N starts it; nothing else does. Its designs are judged exactly like a proposal, and those accepted join the search's finalists and are shown as found by search. Its rejected attempts are kept only in the ledger. The evaluator is frozen. A proposal is accepted only if it differs from every design on the front by a meaningful amount without being beaten by one, and raises the holdout hypervolume by an amount whose 95% interval excludes zero. Every proposal is logged in results/ledger.jsonl with its change on each axis, accepted or not.

Repeated proposals against the same holdout seeds will eventually fit them, and nobody reviews a proposal before it can join the front. So after search.tries_per_holdout new designs have been simulated on a set of holdout seeds (community or agent; re-running a stored proposal does not count), the set counts as used. The next rss-gov try first re-runs the search's finalists on a fresh set and judges them again, then tries every stored proposal again against them (the agent's only if it was accepted). A design that was accepted on luck then drops out.

Tier C: measuring real reactions#

Only a real programme can measure the responses the model has to assume. Each axis has a different status there:

Axis In a real programme
Participation Directly observable: votes and delegation each epoch. Measures drep_response and delegator_response.
Decentralisation Observable, but slow: delegation moves by under 1% an epoch. Expect a direction, not a settled level. Measures reinvest_response.
Skin in the game Only if the programme collects signed account declarations. A pledge soft cap can be tried; that also measures pledge_response.
Cost Known exactly, including the number and size of payments.
Sybil resistance Not directly. Watch for new DReps near the minimum voting power with identical votes, and for large DReps' delegation splitting across new DReps.
  • Pre-register. Before launch, record the rule, the model's predicted change on each axis under each scenario (results/experiment_summary.csv), and the analysis. Afterwards, the scenario closest to the outcome replaces the guess.
  • Randomise. Before/after comparison of turnout is weak: turnout varied by 9 points between actions over the last 80 epochs, so detecting a 5-point change needs about 49 actions per period, more than a six-month programme sees. Randomising who is eligible first is far stronger. A DRep's vote rate changed by 0.16 (standard deviation) between the last two 36-epoch windows, so two cohorts of about 170 DReps detect a 5-point change in vote rate with 80% power, and 45 per cohort detect 10 points.
  • Stagger. Pay cohort A from the start and cohort B from the midpoint. Everyone is paid eventually, and the first half is a clean comparison.
  • Measure what the model predicts. Vote rate per DRep, rationale rate, new registrations, re-delegation toward paid DReps, ADA leaving always-abstain, and declared pledge.

Rules that need a hard fork cannot be tried this way. A soft version of the same rule (a pledge soft cap instead of a hard one) can, and it measures the response a hard cap would rely on.
